Hospitality

AI for hospitality. Fix the booking before anything else.

Hospitality has thin margins and an owner who is usually also working the floor. That combination means automation has to earn its place immediately, and it means the highest-value fix is often not automation at all — it is the booking path that quietly loses people before they ever arrive.

Start with the booking, because it is usually broken

Before any AI, check what actually happens when someone tries to book. Broken widgets, forms that fail on a phone, enquiry emails nobody monitors on a Tuesday — these are extremely common in small venues and cellar doors, and each one loses a booking silently. Nobody complains about a booking form that does not work; they just go elsewhere.

A venue losing bookings to a broken form does not have an AI problem. Fix that first, and measure the difference before adding anything.

Group and event enquiries

Once booking works, the highest-value automation in hospitality is handling group and function enquiries. These are the most valuable bookings and the ones most often lost to slow replies — a wedding party or corporate function enquiry that waits two days has usually already been answered by someone else.

  • Immediate acknowledgement with real availability, not a promise to get back to them.
  • Capturing the details that matter — numbers, date, dietary requirements, budget — before a person gets involved.
  • Following up on quotes sent. Most venues never chase a function quote.

The back of house

  • Rostering against forecast demand, bookings and staff availability — hours of weekly work in most venues.
  • Supplier ordering from stock levels and upcoming bookings.
  • Reconciling supplier invoices against deliveries, where errors are common and rarely caught.
  • Compliance records — food safety logs, temperature records, training documentation.
  • Review responses drafted for the owner to edit. Speed and specificity matter here, and a generic reply is visibly generic to everyone reading.

What guests should never notice

Hospitality sells an experience, and automation that guests can feel undermines the thing they are paying for. A chatbot on the front page of a fine-dining restaurant is a category error. An automated response to a complaint is worse than a slow human one.

Keep it behind the scenes: rosters, ordering, invoices, compliance, internal coordination. The guest-facing exception is booking, where speed genuinely helps them — and even there, a function enquiry should reach a person quickly.

For cellar doors and destination venues specifically, the booking path is frequently the entire problem. Visitors decide in the morning where to go that afternoon; a venue that cannot be booked in two taps on a phone has lost them regardless of how good the wine is.

Common questions

Should a restaurant use an AI chatbot?

Rarely on the front line. Hospitality sells an experience, and a bot answering guests undercuts it. Where automation helps is speed on bookings and function enquiries, and everything behind the scenes — rosters, ordering, invoices, compliance. If a guest can tell, reconsider.

What is the fastest win for a small venue?

Checking that your booking path actually works on a phone, then automating group and function enquiry responses. Function bookings are the most valuable and the most frequently lost to slow replies. Neither requires much technology; both directly affect revenue.

Can AI handle rostering?

It can generate a draft roster from forecast demand, bookings and availability, which removes hours of weekly work. A manager still adjusts it, because the model does not know that one person is training another or that someone needs an early finish. A good draft to edit beats a blank grid.

What about responding to online reviews?

Draft with it, always edit, and never automate sending. A generic reply is obvious to every future customer reading it, and a mishandled response to a genuine complaint does more damage than the complaint. Speed helps; automation without review does not.
