Reputation

Email warm-up, and whether you actually need it.

Warm-up means building a sending history gradually, so that receiving servers learn your domain belongs to a real business before you ask them to deliver anything at volume. A domain with no history sending fifty messages on day one is indistinguishable from a spammer — because that behaviour is exactly what spam looks like.

What is actually happening

Mail providers keep a reputation score for every sending domain. A new domain does not start neutral so much as unknown, and unknown is treated cautiously. Every message that gets delivered, opened and — best of all — replied to, nudges that score upward. Every bounce, every spam complaint, every message deleted unread nudges it down.

Warm-up is simply the process of generating enough positive signal, slowly enough to be believable, that the score is healthy before real volume arrives.

A four-week schedule

  1. Week one, about five a day. Send to people who will actually reply — colleagues, past clients, anyone who knows you. Replies are the strongest positive signal there is.
  2. Week two, ten to fifteen. Begin mixing in real prospects. Watch bounces closely; anything above a couple of percent means your list needs cleaning before you go further.
  3. Week three, twenty to thirty. Mostly real outreach now. Any spam complaint at all is a signal to hold at this level rather than climb.
  4. Week four and beyond, thirty to fifty. Steady state for a single mailbox. Most small businesses never need to exceed this.
Ask people you know to reply, not just to receive. A conversation — message, reply, reply back — is worth far more to your reputation than a message that simply lands and sits there.

Are automated warm-up services worth it?

These services put your mailbox into a network where accounts send and reply to each other automatically, generating artificial positive signal. Bundled into most subscription platforms, they are genuinely useful if you are running many mailboxes — doing that by hand does not scale.

The honest caveat: providers are aware these networks exist and have become better at recognising the pattern of mail that is only ever exchanged inside a closed loop. It is a race, and it is not obviously one the warm-up networks are winning. For a single mailbox at moderate volume, a patient manual warm-up costs nothing and carries no risk of being classified as part of an artificial network.

What warm-up cannot fix

  • Missing authentication. No amount of warm-up compensates for absent SPF, DKIM or DMARC.
  • A bad list. Warming up and then sending to dead addresses undoes the work immediately.
  • Content people report. If recipients mark your mail as spam, reputation falls faster than warm-up can raise it.
  • A domain that is already burnt. Once a domain has a bad history, warming it up rarely works. Starting fresh usually does.

Common questions

How long does warm-up take?

Two to four weeks for a new domain to reach normal outreach volume safely. Faster is possible and regularly ends badly. If a tool promises full volume within days, it is optimising for your impatience rather than your domain.

Do I need to warm up if I am using an established domain?

If the domain has been sending normal business email for years, it already has a reputation and needs far less. But if you are moving from ten messages a day to two hundred, that jump is itself the risk — ramp the increase over a couple of weeks even on an established domain.

Can I warm up and run a real campaign at the same time?

Yes, and it is usually the sensible approach: use the warm-up weeks to send your best-researched, most likely-to-reply prospects. Genuine replies are exactly the signal warm-up is trying to manufacture, so real outreach done slowly warms a domain better than artificial traffic does.

What if my domain is already flagged?

Stop sending, identify what caused it — usually volume, bounces or complaints — and fix that first. Then either warm up again over a longer period, or accept that a genuinely burnt domain is cheaper to replace than to rehabilitate. Twelve dollars for a fresh domain versus weeks of uncertain recovery is not a hard decision.
