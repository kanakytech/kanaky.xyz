Property

AI for real estate. The listing text is the least of it.

Every real estate AI pitch starts with generating listing descriptions, which is the least valuable thing on the list and the easiest to spot. The work that actually consumes an agent's week is vendor communication, buyer follow-up and a database that decays quietly in the background.

Vendor reporting

Vendors want to know what is happening, and agents are worst at communicating during the periods when there is least to report — which is exactly when the anxiety peaks and the relationship is most at risk.

Generating a weekly report from real data — viewings, enquiry numbers, feedback themes, comparable activity — takes minutes instead of an hour per listing. The vendor who receives a regular report chases less and renews the agency agreement more often. Consistency here is worth more than eloquence anywhere else.

The database is the asset nobody works

Most agencies hold thousands of past contacts: buyers who missed out, vendors from years ago, appraisals that never listed. It sits untouched because working it manually is unthinkable.

  • Matching new listings to past buyers on their stated criteria, before the listing goes public.
  • Reactivating old appraisals. Someone who had an appraisal three years ago and did not sell is a live prospect now.
  • Anniversary contact with past vendors — the single highest-conversion outreach in the industry and the one most consistently neglected.
  • Detecting intent signals. A buyer who returns to your site repeatedly is telling you something.

Appraisal preparation

Assembling comparable sales, market data and property history into a presentation is hours of assembly per appraisal. Automating the assembly leaves the agent to do the part that wins the listing — the judgement about price and the conversation in the room.

Where automation damages trust

Real estate is a relationship business operating under conduct rules, and some automation actively harms you:

  • Automated responses to buyer enquiries that read as automated. Buyers making the largest purchase of their lives notice, and it costs you the relationship immediately.
  • Generated market commentary presented as personal insight. If a vendor discovers your market view was assembled rather than considered, everything else you told them is retrospectively suspect.
  • Anything touching disclosure obligations. Material facts about a property are a legal duty. A generated description that misstates one is your problem, not the software's.
The line worth holding: automate the assembly and the remembering, never the judgement or the relationship. An agent whose reports arrive reliably and whose database actually works is more valuable than one with better adjectives.

Common questions

Can AI write property listings?

It can draft them, and the drafts read like drafts unless you supply real detail and edit properly. More importantly, listing text is subject to fair-trading and disclosure rules — a generated description that misstates a material fact is a legal exposure. Draft with it if you like, but read every word before it publishes.

What is the highest-value use for an agency?

Working the existing database. Most agencies hold thousands of past contacts they never contact — old appraisals, buyers who missed out, vendors from years ago. Matching them to new listings and reaching out systematically produces business from people who already know you, which converts far better than any cold approach.

Will automated vendor reports feel impersonal?

Not if they contain real data and you add a genuine comment. A weekly report with actual viewing numbers, enquiry counts and feedback themes plus two sentences of your own reads as attentive. The version that feels impersonal is the one with no data and generic reassurance — which is what vendors currently receive when they receive anything.

Does this work for a single agent?

Particularly well, because a solo agent has no support staff absorbing the reporting and follow-up. The database work in particular scales in a way one person's memory cannot.
