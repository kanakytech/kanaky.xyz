Retail

AI for e-commerce. The catalogue is the opportunity.

E-commerce has a structural problem that suits automation unusually well: the catalogue is always larger than the team. Products go live with thin descriptions, missing attributes and no images beyond the supplier's, because writing properly for two thousand items was never realistic.

Product content at scale

This is where the return is clearest. Generating descriptions, attributes and specifications from supplier data and images turns a catalogue that is 80% thin into one that is complete — which matters for search visibility, for conversion, and for the long tail of products nobody had time to write.

  • Descriptions from supplier data, in your own voice rather than the manufacturer's copy every competitor is also using.
  • Structured attributes extracted consistently, which is what makes filtering and search work properly.
  • Category and tag assignment across thousands of items.
  • Localised versions where you sell across markets.
  • Image tagging for search and merchandising.
A caution that matters commercially: identical manufacturer copy across many retailers is a known weakness in search, and thin generated content is not obviously better. Content should add something real — fit, use, comparison, what it suits — or it is volume without value. Generate to a brief, not to a word count.

Customer support

Support deflection is the other obvious application, and the one most often done badly. Order status, delivery timeframes, returns policy and stock questions are genuinely repetitive and can be handled well.

What ruins it is a bot that cannot escalate. A customer with a real problem trapped in a loop is worse than a slow email reply, and it is the single most common complaint about e-commerce automation. Build the escape hatch first, make it obvious, and measure how often people use it — high escape rates are telling you something about the bot, not about the customers.

Behind the storefront

  • Demand forecasting for purchasing, particularly on seasonal lines.
  • Supplier communication — purchase orders, chasing, discrepancy resolution.
  • Review analysis at volume, surfacing product problems before they show up in returns data.
  • Returns processing and pattern detection — repeated returns on one product usually indicate a description or sizing problem you can fix.
  • Abandoned cart recovery with messages referencing the actual product rather than a template.

Common questions

Can AI write product descriptions that rank in search?

It can produce descriptions at catalogue scale, which is valuable mainly because the alternative is thin or duplicated manufacturer copy. Whether they rank depends on whether they add something real — fit, use, comparison, suitability. Generating volume for its own sake is the version search engines have become good at discounting.

Should I use an AI chatbot for customer support?

For order status, delivery, returns and stock questions, yes — those are repetitive and well handled. The requirement is an obvious, easy escalation to a person. A customer with a real problem stuck in a loop costs you more than the support hours you saved, and it is the most common failure in e-commerce automation.

What is the fastest win for a small online store?

Completing the catalogue. Most small stores have hundreds of products with thin descriptions and missing attributes, which suppresses both search visibility and conversion across the long tail. Fixing it manually was never going to happen; fixing it in a week is now realistic.

Can AI help with product photography?

Image generation and editing can produce lifestyle context and format variants from existing product shots at a fraction of traditional cost. The constraint is honesty: the product shown must be the product sold. Generated imagery that misrepresents an item is a fair-trading problem, not a creative decision.
