Trades

AI for electricians. Mostly, it is the quoting.

The pattern in electrical contracting is consistent enough to name: the work finishes at five, and the paperwork starts at eight. Quotes get written at the kitchen table, compliance forms pile up, and the enquiry that came in at eleven gets a reply on Thursday — by which time someone else has already quoted it.

Quoting is where the hours are

A quote for a switchboard upgrade or a rewire is not intellectually hard; it is a known set of materials, a labour estimate and a callout. What makes it expensive is that it happens after a full day of physical work, when nobody wants to open a laptop.

Dictating the job into a phone in the van and getting back a formatted, priced quote changes the economics of that entirely — not because the quote is better, but because it goes out the same evening instead of the following week. In trades, speed of response is frequently what decides the job, and the tradie who quotes on the day wins work from the one who quotes properly on Friday.

The compliance layer

Certificates of compliance, electrical safety certificates, records of testing — jurisdiction-dependent, repetitive, and legally required. AI does not sign these and should not, but it can populate the routine fields from job details you already recorded, leaving you to check and sign rather than transcribe.

Where else it pays

  • Enquiry response. An immediate acknowledgement with a realistic timeframe, so the customer stops phoning the next electrician on the list.
  • Job history search. "What did we charge for the same board at the Kelburn place" answered in seconds from your own records.
  • Materials lists generated from a job description, for pricing or ordering.
  • Chasing the quotes you sent. Most electricians never follow up a quote. A follow-up four days later is close to free and converts a meaningful share of silence.
  • Finding commercial work. Property managers, builders and facilities companies are reachable, and almost nobody in the trade approaches them systematically.

What is not worth it

Anything customer-facing that pretends to be you. A chatbot that answers technical questions about their wiring is a liability, not an efficiency — customers want an electrician's judgement, and a confident wrong answer about electrical safety is worse than no answer. Keep AI in the paperwork, the admin and the chasing.

The realistic prize is one to two hours an evening returned. Not a transformed business — an evening back, and quotes going out while the job is still fresh in the customer's mind.

Common questions

Can AI write my quotes?

It can produce a formatted, priced draft from a spoken description of the job — line items, labour, callout, tax — that you check and send. It does not know your margins or your suppliers unless you tell it, and it should never send a quote without you seeing it. The saving is in not typing at nine at night, which is most of the cost.

Will it handle my compliance certificates?

It can fill routine fields from job data you already have, which removes re-keying. It cannot certify work and must not be trusted to decide what is compliant — that is your registration and your liability. Treat it as a form-filler that you verify and sign.

Is this worth it for a one-person operation?

Often more so than for a larger firm, because a sole operator has no office person absorbing the admin. The evening hours you get back are your own. Start with quoting alone rather than trying to automate everything at once.

How do I find commercial clients?

Property managers, building companies, facilities managers and real estate firms all need reliable electrical contractors and are reachable by email. Very few electricians approach them systematically, which is precisely why it works — see B2B lead generation for small businesses.
