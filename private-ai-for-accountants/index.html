Regulated sectors

AI in an accounting practice without exposing client financials.

Accounting practices hold concentrated financial information about a great many businesses. That makes AI unusually valuable — the work is repetitive, document-heavy and rule-based — and unusually sensitive. Running the model in-house resolves the tension directly.

Professional obligations come first. Confidentiality requirements from your professional body, and the terms of your engagement letters, govern what you may do with client data. This page describes what is technically possible, not what your standards permit.

Where the payback is fastest

  • Document extraction. Pulling figures, dates and parties out of invoices, receipts, statements and contracts into structured form. Repetitive, high volume, and exactly what these models do well.
  • Transaction classification. Sorting entries into accounts, learning from how you have categorised similar items before.
  • Reconciliation support. Surfacing likely matches and flagging what does not fit, for a human to resolve.
  • Drafting client correspondence from a file — explanatory letters, queries, follow-ups.
  • Searching your own history. "How did we treat this for a similar client" answered from your own files rather than memory.
  • Deadline and obligation tracking extracted from documents rather than re-keyed.

Why in-house makes sense here

The concentration is the point. A single practice may hold detailed financial records for hundreds of businesses — turnover, margins, debts, disputes, salaries. That is a more attractive target and a more consequential exposure than any single business faces on its own.

Sending that material to an external service may be permissible under the right terms; keeping it inside means the question of a provider breach, a terms change, or a sub-processor you did not evaluate simply does not arise.

A realistic setup

1
One capable machine in the practice — sizing guidance here.
2
Integration with your practice software, so extracted data lands where it is used rather than in a separate silo.
3
Client-level access control. Staff should only be able to query clients they work on, exactly as with your document system.
4
Human review on anything that affects a return or a set of accounts. These models are fluent and occasionally confidently wrong — the review step is not optional.
5
An audit trail of what was generated and by whom.

What it does not do

It does not exercise professional judgement, and it should not be trusted to apply tax law unverified. Its value is in the mechanical layer — reading, extracting, sorting, drafting — which happens to be where a large share of billable hours currently disappears. That is a substantial prize without needing to overstate it.

Common questions

Can accountants use AI with client data?

Subject to your professional body's confidentiality requirements and your engagement terms, generally yes — but the tier and terms matter. Many practices are comfortable with business-tier tools under appropriate contracts; others conclude that concentrated client financial data should not leave the practice. Running the model in-house makes that a settled question rather than a judgement call.

Which tasks pay back fastest?

Document extraction and transaction classification, by a wide margin. They are high-volume, repetitive, and currently consume junior hours that could go to work clients value more. Start there rather than with anything requiring judgement.

Is a local model accurate enough for financial documents?

For extracting figures and classifying transactions, yes — with verification. Treat it as a fast first pass that a person checks, not as an authority. The efficiency comes from reviewing extracted data rather than keying it, which is still a large saving.

What about during peak season?

Peak is when the volume argument is strongest — and when hardware capacity, rather than a per-request bill, becomes the constraint. Size the machine for your busiest month rather than your average one; the cost difference is smaller than the cost of a system that queues when you need it most.
