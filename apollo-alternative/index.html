Comparison

An Apollo alternative when you do not need the database.

Apollo bundles two products: a large contact database and a sequencing tool. Whether it is worth its subscription depends almost entirely on whether the database is the thing you actually need — and for a lot of local and trade-focused businesses, it is not.

On this page
  1. What you are really paying for
  2. The comparison
  3. Where Apollo is the better choice
  4. Where finding companies yourself wins

What you are really paying for

Apollo's value is the data: millions of contacts, filterable by industry, size, technology and role. For selling software to companies in defined categories, that is genuinely useful and hard to replicate.

It is much less useful when your market is geographic and local. Independent boatbuilders in Northland, electricians in Christchurch, wineries on Waiheke — these businesses are often absent, out of date, or represented by a generic address in large databases. The good data for them is on their own websites and in trade directories.

We have not verified Apollo's current pricing tiers and are not going to quote figures we have not checked. Their pricing page is the authority. What we can compare is the model: a recurring subscription for access to data and sequencing, versus a one-time purchase of a system that finds companies itself.

Where Apollo is the better choice

  • You sell into well-covered categories. Software, mid-market and enterprise companies are exactly what large databases index well.
  • You filter on firmographics or technology. Company size, funding, tech stack — that filtering is the product, and building it yourself is not realistic.
  • You need volume of contacts immediately. A database gives you thousands today; researching companies yourself is slower.
  • You need multiple named contacts per company. Finding the right person inside a large organisation is what the data is for.

Where finding companies yourself wins

  • Local and trade markets. These are poorly covered in aggregate databases and well covered by their own websites and trade registers.
  • Freshness. A company's own site today beats a database record from an unknown date.
  • Sourcing transparency. You know exactly where each address came from, which matters legally — see finding business emails ethically and the Australian rules in particular.
  • Cost over time. Data subscriptions are typically the largest recurring line in an outbound stack.

Common questions

Does the Cold Outreach Machine include a contact database?

No. It finds companies matching the profile you describe, from public sources, at the time you run it — rather than querying a pre-built database. For local and trade markets that produces fresher and more complete results; for filtering large organisations by firmographics, a database is the better instrument.

Can I import lists I already have?

Yes. Existing lists import as CSV, and everything you import is checked against the permanent contact registry so nobody is approached twice across campaigns.

Is data from a database legally safer than finding it yourself?

Not inherently, and often the opposite. Under Australian and European rules in particular, what matters is your lawful basis and your ability to show where data came from. A database record whose origin the vendor will not detail is harder to defend than an address you took from the company's own published contact page.

What if my market is not in any database?

That is the case this is built for, and it is more common than the tooling market suggests. Most local trades, marine businesses, hospitality operators and regional service companies are thinly covered in aggregate data but publish everything you need on their own sites.
