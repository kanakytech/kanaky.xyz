Sourcing

Finding business emails without buying a problem.

Where your list comes from determines your bounce rate, your spam complaints, your legal exposure and ultimately your domain reputation. It is the least glamorous part of outbound and the one that most reliably decides whether any of it works.

On this page
  1. Sources that hold up
  2. Sources that create problems
  3. Verification is not optional
  4. The part that matters legally

Sources that hold up

  • The company website. Contact pages, team pages, service pages. Published deliberately for people to make contact — which is exactly what you are doing.
  • Business directories and trade associations. Industry bodies, chambers of commerce, licensing registers. Businesses list themselves there specifically to be found.
  • Company registers. Public filings in most countries include registered contact details.
  • Job listings. Often name a hiring contact, and reveal what the business is currently struggling to cover — useful for both reaching and understanding them.
  • Professional networks, used within their terms of service. Read those terms; automated scraping is generally prohibited and enforced.

Sources that create problems

  • Purchased lists. Stale, unverified, frequently containing spam traps, and the seller usually cannot tell you where the data came from. Bounce rates from bought lists are the fastest route to a flagged domain.
  • Data from breaches. Unlawful in essentially every jurisdiction, regardless of how it is packaged or resold.
  • Scraped personal profiles. Personal email addresses and personal social data carry the strongest privacy protections and the weakest business justification.
  • Guessed addresses at volume. Pattern-guessing firstname@company.com and firing at all variants produces bounces and hits catch-all traps. Verification exists precisely so you do not have to guess.

Verification is not optional

Every address should be checked before it is sent to. Verification services confirm that a mailbox exists without sending anything, and they cost very little relative to the damage a high bounce rate does.

  1. Remove anything that does not resolve. Non-existent addresses bounce, and bounce rate feeds directly into reputation.
  2. Treat catch-all domains carefully. These accept everything, so verification cannot confirm a real mailbox. Send to them sparingly, or find a named contact instead.
  3. Drop role addresses for cold outreach — info@, sales@, admin@. They are frequently monitored by several people, ignored, or configured as complaint traps. A named person is both more effective and safer.
  4. Re-verify old lists. Contact data decays quickly as people change roles. A list gathered six months ago is not the list you have today.

The part that matters legally

Sourcing rules differ by jurisdiction and are stricter than most outreach advice admits. In the European Union and the United Kingdom, personal data — which includes named business email addresses — falls under data protection law, and you need a lawful basis plus, in practice, a privacy notice explaining where you got it. Australia's Privacy Act limits using data for purposes other than why it was collected. New Zealand and the United States are more permissive for business-to-business contact but still require genuine sender identity and a working opt-out.

A reliable filter that keeps you out of most trouble: would this business be surprised that you have this address? A contact address published on their own website, no. A personal address obtained from a data broker, yes — and that surprise is what complaints are made of.

Common questions

Is it legal to scrape business emails from websites?

It depends on jurisdiction and on what you scrape. Publicly published business contact details are generally acceptable for business-to-business contact in New Zealand, Australia and the United States, subject to identification and opt-out rules. In the EU and UK, named business addresses are personal data and require a lawful basis and transparency about the source. Automated scraping may also breach a site's terms of service independently of privacy law.

Should I use email finder tools?

The reputable ones aggregate public sources and verify deliverability, which is legitimate and saves real time. What matters is where their data comes from — a provider that cannot tell you is one whose data you should not use. Verify anything they give you before sending regardless.

What bounce rate is acceptable?

Under two percent. Above that, providers begin treating you as someone sending to an unverified list, which is exactly what is happening. If a campaign starts bouncing heavily, stop it rather than letting it run — every additional bounce compounds the damage.

Are role addresses like info@ worth contacting?

Rarely, for cold outreach. They are often monitored by several people, frequently ignored, and sometimes configured specifically to catch unsolicited mail. A named person at the same company is more likely to reply and less likely to generate a complaint.
