What cold outreach actually costs. The software is the small number.

Every comparison of cold email tools argues about the monthly fee. It's the wrong argument. The fee is real, but it is nowhere near the largest cost of doing outbound — and the largest one never appears in anybody's pricing table.

Here is the whole picture, at July 2026 prices, for a small business or consultant sending a steady twenty emails a day.

The line items everyone compares

Software. Subscription platforms sit between roughly US$39 and US$97 a month for one user — Instantly from about $37, Smartlead from about $39, lemlist from about $39 and rising steeply per seat. Over a year: somewhere between $470 and $1,160. Perpetual-licence alternatives exist and are bought once: self-hosted senders like AcelleMail (from ~$80), Cleanmails (~$199) or Broadcast (~$250), and systems that also find and write, like our own Cold Outreach Machine ($299.99).

A sending domain. About $12 a year. It should not be your main company domain — if deliverability ever suffers, you want the damage contained somewhere that isn't where your invoices come from.

Sending infrastructure. An SMTP or email service, from free tiers up to $20-odd a month at this volume, plus the DNS work to verify SPF, DKIM and DMARC. That verification is not optional: without it, most of what you send goes to spam regardless of which software you bought.

AI writing, if you use it. A few cents per email through a cloud provider — at twenty a day, roughly $10 to $25 a month. Or nothing per email if you run a local model on your own hardware, which also keeps prospect data off third-party servers.

Add it up and a realistic year runs from about $400 at the frugal end to about $1,500 at the comfortable end. That's the number people optimise. Now the one they don't.

The line nobody puts in the spreadsheet

Doing outbound properly means, for each company: finding it, checking it actually fits, locating the right person, reading enough about the business to say something specific and true, and writing a message that doesn't read like a template. Done honestly, that is eight to twelve minutes per company. Not because writing is slow, but because the reading that makes the writing worth sending is slow.

Twenty a day, five days a week:

Per day2h40 – 4h00
Per week13 – 20 hours
Per yearroughly 650 – 1,000 hours

Price those hours at anything at all — even a modest $30 — and outbound costs tens of thousands a year in time, against software measured in hundreds. The subscription argument is a rounding error inside the real number.

Which is why, in practice, most small businesses don't do outbound at all. Not from a strategic decision: they simply never find fifteen hours a week, so it gets postponed until a quiet month makes it urgent, then done in a panicked burst, then dropped again. The burst is also the version most likely to get a domain flagged, because panic sends fast.

What this means for the buying decision

If a tool only sends — you still build the list and write every message — then you've bought delivery, and the 650 to 1,000 hours stay exactly where they were. That can be the right purchase; just be clear it isn't the expensive part of the problem.

The purchase worth thinking about is whatever removes hours. Automating sending saves minutes. Automating finding and writing is what turns outbound from a job into a background process. And it's the harder thing to build, which is why fewer tools do it.

There's a caveat that deserves stating: automated writing is only as good as the description of who you're targeting. "Marine businesses" produces forgettable email. "Independent boatbuilders in Northland" produces messages that sound like someone looked. The machine removes the hours; it doesn't remove the thinking about who's worth writing to.

The short version

Software: hundreds a year. Infrastructure: about $12 for a domain plus sending, and non-negotiable DNS records. Time: the actual cost, by an order of magnitude, and the only line worth optimising hard.

Compare tools on how many of those hours they give you back — not on the monthly fee.
